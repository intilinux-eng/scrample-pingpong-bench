Does zero-copy IPC stay constant from 64 B to 10 MiB?

Measurements of score::mw::com, the shared-memory middleware of Eclipse S-CORE, on a Linux laptop. Each section explains what a number means and why it looks the way it does, so the page can be read as a lesson as well as a report.

Read these numbers as a learning run, not a performance claim. The benchmark ran pinned to fixed cores but under the normal Linux scheduler (no SCHED_FIFO, no core isolation, no memory locking) on a laptop with a desktop session open. The shape of each curve is trustworthy; the tail values (p99.9, max) would be much smaller on a tuned real-time machine. See what would make the numbers count.

New, 14 November 2026: S-CORE v0.10.0. Sections 1–6 describe the first run, made with S-CORE platform release v0.5.0-alpha (score_communication 0.1.1). The benchmark now builds against v0.10.0 (score_communication 0.6.0). Section 7 compares the two versions on the same machine: the zero-copy curve is still flat, and one round trip in a single process is about 23 % faster.

1. The experiment in one paragraph

A Master writes a ping into a shared-memory slot and publishes it on Topic_A. A Reflector is notified, reads the ping, and publishes a pong on Topic_B. The Master measures the round-trip time (RTT) from Send() until the pong arrives. This repeats tens of thousands of times for each of seven payload sizes, 64 B to 10 MiB (each step ×8, the last ×5). Three variants separate what the middleware costs from what the application's own memory work costs:

  • BM_PingPong_NoFill — the payload bytes are never written. Only the transfer is measured. If the transfer is truly zero-copy, this curve must be flat.
  • BM_PingPong — the Master writes all N bytes just before starting the clock, like an application that has just produced its data.
  • BM_PingPong_FullEcho — the write of N bytes is inside the timed region, and the Reflector copies all N bytes back. This is the literal "send the same data back" flow, so it must grow with N.

Lesson 1: zero-copy really is O(1)

The blue line (NoFill) stays flat at about 6.6 µs in one process and about 13.5 µs in two processes, whether the slot carries 64 bytes or 10 MiB. Google Benchmark fitted O(1) with an RMS error of 1 % in both topologies. That is the answer to the question at the top of the page: yes.

Why it works

A copying transport (a socket, a pipe) moves every byte through the kernel, so it pays O(N). mw::com's LoLa binding instead lets the sender Allocate() a slot that already lives in shared memory, write into it in place, and Send() only a small notification that says "slot k is ready". The receiver gets a SamplePtr that points into the same physical memory. No payload byte moves, so the size cannot matter.

Lesson 2: "not timed" does not mean "free"

The orange line (BM_PingPong) runs the same transfer, yet it climbs at the largest sizes: 6.6 µs → 8.0 µs at 2 MiB → 13.8 µs at 10 MiB in one process, and about 14 µs → 31.9 µs at 10 MiB in two processes. The only difference from NoFill is the memset() that runs before the clock starts.

Why it happens

This CPU has 1 MiB of L2 cache per core and 16 MiB of shared L3. Writing 2 MiB or 10 MiB streams through the caches and pushes out everything else, including the middleware's own control data, the thread stacks and the condition variable the benchmark waits on. The next round trip then finds them in slower cache levels or in RAM, and the dirty payload lines may still be draining to memory. Earlier work shapes later latency. In a real system, a big producer on the same core makes a small, latency-critical message slower even when nothing is copied.

Look at the fitted complexity for this benchmark: O(log N) with an RMS error of about 180 %. A fit that bad means "no simple curve matches". It is not evidence of logarithmic behaviour. Only trust a big-O fit when its RMS is small.

Lesson 3: the application's memory work is O(N), and has cache cliffs

The aqua line (FullEcho) is flat up to 4 KiB, because writing and copying a few kilobytes takes well under a microsecond next to the ~7 µs fixed cost. Above that it grows with N, reaching about 1 ms at 10 MiB. That millisecond is the memset plus the Reflector's memcpy, not the middleware: the NoFill line proves the transfer itself costs the same 6.6 µs.

Do the arithmetic

2 MiB → 10 MiB is 5× more data, but the RTT goes from about 129 µs to about 1 048 µs, 8× more. At 2 MiB the fill and the copy (about 6 MiB of traffic) still fit in the 16 MiB L3 cache, which gives roughly 50 GB/s. At 10 MiB the source and destination together (20 MiB) no longer fit, so the traffic goes to DRAM at roughly 30 GB/s. The CPU-time column of BM_PingPong shows the same cliff for the memset alone: about 24 µs for 2 MiB (in cache), about 320 µs for 10 MiB (to RAM). Memory bandwidth is not one number; it depends on where the data lives. That is also why the O(N log N) fit for this benchmark (RMS about 17 %) is a curve-fitting artefact: the real behaviour is "O(N), with a steeper slope once the data falls out of L3".

Lesson 4: crossing a process boundary costs about 7 µs per round trip

Switch the chart to Two processes: every curve moves up by the same amount, from about 6.6 µs to about 13.5 µs at small sizes, and the shapes stay the same. Shared memory removes the copy, but the notification still has to reach the other process.

Why it happens

In the mw::com source (message_passing_service_instance.cpp), a subscriber in the same process is notified by calling its receive handler directly (NotifyEventLocally). A subscriber in another process gets a message through a POSIX message queue (score/mw/com/message_passing/mqueue). That costs system calls and a thread wake-up in the receiving process, and a round trip crosses the boundary twice. A first guess was that each process, pinned to a single core, made its own threads compete for that core. A control run with two cores per process measured the same 14.0–14.6 µs, which rules that out. Testing a hypothesis is cheap; guessing is not.

3. Tail latency: the number real-time systems care about

Median over 5 repetitions of each percentile. Logarithmic axis.

Show the numbers as a table

The typical one-way latency (p50, the median) is about 3.2 µs in one process. 99 % of messages arrive within about 4.5 µs, and 99.9 % within about 7 µs. But the slowest message of a run takes tens to hundreds of microseconds, and in some runs well over 1 ms. The mean hides this completely: thousands of fast samples swamp a handful of slow ones.

Notice that the two lines cross at the max: the single-process run, faster at every percentile, had the slower worst sample. A max is one sample out of about 100 000. It records whatever happened to disturb the run, not a property of the topology. That is why real-time validation runs for hours and reports the max together with the run length.

Why it matters

An airbag or a brake controller does not care about the average; it cares about the worst case. Under the normal scheduler, any other task, interrupt or kernel thread can take the core away from the benchmark for a while, and a page fault on first touch can add more. Those are the events that create the long tail. The real-time recipe in docs/04 (isolated cores, SCHED_FIFO, mlockall, a fixed CPU frequency) attacks exactly these causes. It should shrink the max far more than the p50.

5. How the run was made

Reproduce it from a clone of the repository (Linux x86-64, GCC and Bazelisk installed):

# build: --config=host carries the S-CORE feature flags (see .bazelrc)
bazel build --config=host -c opt //src/benchmark:score_pingpong_bench
BIN=bazel-bin/src/benchmark/score_pingpong_bench
CFG=$PWD/src/benchmark/etc/mw_com_config.json

# single process, pinned to cores 2 and 3
taskset -c 2,3 $BIN --role=both --service_instance_manifest=$CFG \
  --benchmark_repetitions=5 --benchmark_out=single.json --benchmark_out_format=json

# two processes: reflector on core 3, master on core 2
taskset -c 3 $BIN --role=reflector --service_instance_manifest=$CFG &
taskset -c 2 $BIN --role=master --service_instance_manifest=$CFG \
  --benchmark_repetitions=5 --benchmark_out=split.json --benchmark_out_format=json
kill %1

# condense for this page
tools/summarise_results.py --label "single process" single.json \
  --label "two processes" split.json --out site/data/results.json

Pick two cores that are different physical cores: on this CPU, logical CPUs 2 and 10 are the two hyper-threads of one core, so 2 and 3 are used. Check yours with cat /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu2/topology/thread_siblings_list.

6. What would make the numbers count

  1. Boot with isolcpus=2,3 nohz_full=2,3 rcu_nocbs=2,3 so that nothing else runs on the measurement cores.
  2. Run src/benchmark/run_benchmark.sh. It adds chrt -f 99 (SCHED_FIFO) and, as root, mlockall.
  3. Set the performance CPU governor and plug in the laptop. This run used powersave with the balance_performance energy preference.
  4. Check the machine itself with cyclictest on the same cores, so that a bad tail can be blamed on the machine or the middleware, not guessed.

Expected effect: the p50 hardly moves, while p99.9 and max should drop sharply. Seeing that happen is the best way to learn what real-time tuning actually buys.

7. S-CORE v0.10.0 against v0.5.0-alpha

The benchmark moved from S-CORE platform release v0.5.0-alpha (score_communication 0.1.1, score_baselibs 0.1.3) to v0.10.0 (score_communication 0.6.0, score_baselibs 0.2.17). Did a year of upstream work change the cost of a transfer? Both binaries ran on the same laptop with the same settings, one after the other, alternating versions so that slow drift (temperature, background load) hits both alike. The whole comparison was made twice; this page shows the second pass, made with the exact binary of the updated repository.

Lesson 5: zero-copy is still O(1), and the fixed cost went down

Both versions stay flat from 64 B to 10 MiB, and Google Benchmark fits O(1) with an RMS error of 1–2 % for all four runs. What changed is the height of the line. In one process the round trip dropped from about 6.7 µs to 5.2 µs, 22–25 % at every payload size, in both passes. Across two processes it dropped from about 13.4 µs to 12.2 µs; the typical one-way latency (p50) went from about 6.6 µs to 6.0 µs, and the direction was the same in both passes.

What is not known yet

Reading the source does not explain the gain. The notification path follows the same algorithm in both versions: the code moved from NotifyEventHandler to MessagePassingServiceInstance, but it still sends to remote subscribers first and then hands local receive handlers to a pool of two worker threads. The difference may sit in score_baselibs (thread pool, synchronisation), in the read path (GetNewSamples), or even in compiler settings that newer Bazel rules bring. A perf profile of both binaries is the next step. Measuring a difference and explaining it are separate tasks.

The tails (p99.9 and max) moved in both directions between the two passes, and the first pass showed FullEcho at 2 MiB across two processes 50 % slower, which the second pass did not repeat. Under the normal scheduler such numbers mostly record disturbances, so they are not used to compare versions here. The full tables are in the results log, and the raw data for this section is in data/compare.json.
